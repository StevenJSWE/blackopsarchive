## Overview

Operation Mockingbird was a covert CIA program that began in the late 1940s under the Office of Policy Coordination and continued in various forms through at least the 1970s. Its objective was to influence domestic and foreign media by recruiting journalists, editors, and executives at major outlets to publish stories favorable to CIA interests, discredit targets, and suppress stories damaging to the Agency.

The effort grew out of the CIA's early postwar propaganda and political warfare mission. Frank Wisner, head of the Office of Policy Coordination, and later Allen Dulles viewed the press as a key instrument of influence both abroad and, to a lesser extent, at home.

## Scale

At its height, the program maintained assets or cooperative relationships inside virtually every major American media outlet — including the New York Times, Washington Post, Time, Newsweek, CBS, NBC, and the Associated Press. Journalist Carl Bernstein's 1977 Rolling Stone investigation, drawing on CIA files obtained after the Church Committee and on-the-record interviews with Agency officials, identified more than 400 American journalists who had secretly carried out assignments for the CIA over the preceding twenty-five years.

The Church Committee later documented at least 25 American journalists who were formally on the CIA payroll, plus a larger number who received expenses, tasking, or other forms of support without being full "employees."

## Methods

- Planting fabricated or selectively shaped stories through recruited journalists and stringers
- Funding or subsidizing front publications, news services, and radio stations abroad (often in Europe and Latin America)
- Using domestic media assets to attack or discredit individuals and movements the CIA viewed as threats
- Coordinating with foreign media networks for international propaganda and disinformation operations
- Providing "background" briefings and leaked documents to favored correspondents to shape coverage of sensitive events

## Church Committee Findings

The 1975–1977 Senate Church Committee (Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities) confirmed that the CIA had maintained systematic paid and unpaid relationships with American journalists and several major news organizations. CIA Director William Colby testified during the hearings that the Agency still maintained "a few" journalists on its payroll at the time of the investigation. The committee's final report detailed the 25 journalists formally carried on CIA books and described broader efforts to influence reporting through "stringers," part-time correspondents, and editorial executives.

The committee also examined the CIA's use of the press in specific operations, including attempts to influence coverage of the Bay of Pigs invasion (where several outlets withheld or softened stories at the Agency's request), the Vietnam War, and domestic dissent. Bernstein noted that the relationships were not limited to stringers; some involved senior editors and executives who facilitated placement of stories or killed others. The Church Committee report emphasized that these activities violated the CIA's charter prohibition on domestic operations, although the Agency maintained that the relationships were primarily "foreign intelligence" in nature.

## Post-Church Developments

In the immediate aftermath of the hearings, the CIA announced new restrictions on relationships with American media. Subsequent directors, including Stansfield Turner, stated that the Agency had ended paid relationships with full-time U.S. journalists. Critics have continued to allege ongoing informal contacts and influence, but the formal, large-scale program described in the 1970s investigations was curtailed.

## Status

**Confirmed.** The existence of sustained CIA efforts to recruit and task American and foreign journalists is documented in Church Committee testimony, declassified CIA records, and the 1977 investigations by Carl Bernstein and others. The precise number of assets and the full extent of influence operations remain partially classified; the CIA has never released a complete list of all individuals involved. The core facts of media penetration during the Cold War are no longer disputed by official sources.
